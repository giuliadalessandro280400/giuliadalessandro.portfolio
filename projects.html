~MADE FOR TRAVEL.~

Comfort and functionality for orchestra members

SEPT ‘25 FEB ‘26

created in Milan, from Rome to Prague

✴ ♯A fashion collection that reimagines the traditional attire of orchestra musicians through the lens of mobility, performance, and modern comfort.
Orchestra players, physical strain in black tie formality.

✴ ♯Each piece pays homage to the elegant, tailored codes of formal wear, fusing them with technical fabrics and design solutions engineered for sharpness and sportswear-like ease.

Whether on a plane, in a practice room, under stage lights, or seated at a post-concert dinner, the garments are made to move, breathe, and transition fluidly with the musician’s rhythm.

/design.

Armonica is defined by a distinctive pleated construction, developed to create volume, flexibility and controlled shape.

Each pleat is secured with topstitching on both the outside and inside, allowing the garment to move freely while maintaining structure — preventing the pleats from opening too wide or losing their form. This technique enhances mobility and comfort, while preserving a precise, sartorial finish.

The construction is featured in the sleeves of a women’s trench coat and a men’s jacket, where stitching runs through the centre of each fold, and in a men’s shirt and women’s trousers, where stitching follows the pleat edges for a sharper, more graphic definition.

Concertech is the performance-driven line designed for orchestral musicians on stage.

The silhouettes are classic and formal — refined black pieces that read like traditional concertwear from a distance — yet they are engineered with advanced technical fabrics. Stretch, breathability, thermoregulation, odour control and wrinkle resistance are integrated to support long performances, travel, and the physical demands of playing.

Each garment combines elegant tailoring with discreet functional details, offering a professional concert look with the comfort and reliability of technical wear.

AIDA FLECHA
ANH THU HUYNH
GIORGIA MONTICCIOLO
GIULIA D’ALESSANDRO

A PROJECT ABOUT
BIOMATERIALS

WINDOW explores how a bio-plastic material, reminiscent of alabaster, can transform through light and movement. The garment is a large modular circle, halfway between a cape and a stained-glass window, which can be worn in different ways to play with light and shadow on the body. Through patchwork, boning, and ribbons that the wearer pulls and shapes, the material becomes wearable architecture: a small luminous building that filters the outside world and turns the body into part of the structure itself, balancing lightness and weight, opacity and transparency.

“PORTRAIT, 1990 – I wanted the model to look as if she had just
stepped out of a portrait. I put her on a pedestal, perched on two diz-
zying platforms. Every fabric had to be an epitome, from lace to velvet,
to tartan and rustic tweeds: the whole gamut, to then finish on canvas,
because François Boucher’s The Sleeping Shepherdess was featured on
the ‘Statue of Liberty’ and the stretch velvet was adorned with motifs
taken from furniture in the Wallace Collection. The ‘Portrait’ hat from
the collection was a high felt crown with a narrow brim, suited to any
garment since the beginnings of oil painting.”

Vivienne Westwood about SS90 PORTRAIT, Fury, A. (2021). Vivienne
Westwood: Catwalk - The Complete Collections. Thames & Hudson.

“Since the dialectic between what’s said and what’s seen is irony’s native land, the most
classic television irony functions via the juxtaposition of audio and video that don’t ma-
tch. What’s seen disproves what’s said. A scholarly article on network news describes a
famous interview with a corporate guy from United Fruit on a CBS special about Guate-
mala: “I sure don’t know of anybody being so-called ‘oppressed,’” this guy, in a ‘70s leisure
suit and bad comb-over, tells Ed Rabel. “I think this is just something that some reporters
have thought up.” The whole interview is intercut with commentless footage of big-bellied
kids in Guatemalan slums and union leaders lying with their throats cut in the mud.”

Wallace, D. F. (1993). E unibus pluram: Television and U.S. fiction. Review of Contempo-
rary Fiction, 13(2), 151–194.

“In the collection of the Musée Historique des Tissus in
Lyons, Westwood examined a rare surviving specimen of a
pourpoint – a fourteenth-century quilted doublet designed
to be worn under plate armour, its front padded to fill out a
convex breastplate and with a large grande assiette armho-
le that allowed 360-degree arm movement. These archaic
details – specifically the sleeve construction – were transla-
ted by Westwood into strikingly futuristic cuts that she and
Kronthaler continue to use to this day.”

Fury, A. (2021). Vivienne Westwood: Catwalk - The Comple-
te Collections. Thames & Hudson.

“The next real literary “rebels” in this country might well

emerge as some weird bunch of anti-rebels, born oglers who
dare somehow to back away from ironic watching, who have
the childish gall actually to endorse and instantiate sin-
gle-pleated values. Who treat of plain old untrendy human
troubles and emotions in U.S. life with reverence and convi-
ction. Who eschew self-consciousness and hip fatigue. These
anti-rebels would be outdated, of course, before they even
started. Dead on the page. Too sincere. Clearly repressed.
Backward, quaint, naive, anachronistic. Maybe that’ll be the
point. Maybe that’s why they’ll be the next real rebels. Real
rebels, as far as I can see, risk disapproval. The old post-
modern insurgents risked the gasp and the squeal: shock,
disgust, outrage, censorship, accusations of socialism, anar-
chism, nihilism. Today’s risks are different. The new rebels
might be artists willing to risk the yawn, the rolled eye, the
cool smile, the nudged rib, the parody of gifted ironists, the
“Oh, how banal.” To risk accusations of sentimentality, me-
lodrama. Of overcredulity. Of softness. Of willingness to be
fooled by a world of dedicated looking-handlers who count
on art’s ironic distance from mediocre reality to make the
whole mess feel tolerable.”
